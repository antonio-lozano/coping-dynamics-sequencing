## Automated freezing detection shows blunted freezing progression in ELS offspring

Our first goal was to establish a reliable, objective measure of freezing behavior using a supervised learning approach. Since manual scoring is time consuming and prone to observer bias, we used SimBA¹² to train a random forest classifier on hand-labeled freezing events and features generated from DeepLabCut¹¹ pose estimation data. Once trained, the model was tested on an independent, held-out manually annotated set of 211,500 frames, where its predictions closely matched human scoring (r = 0.95, R² = 0.90, p < 0.001, Fig. 1). This strong correlation supports using the classifier's output as ground truth for all further analyses.

We next examined how a history of ELS affects the progression of freezing over time during FC using two published datasets²⁴,²⁵. To isolate the effects of ELS itself, only control and ELS groups were included, excluding any further treatment or experimental variables. A mixed linear model analysis confirmed a significant main effect of time in both cohorts (Sanguino-Gómez and Krugers, 2024: β = 3.721, SE = 0.128, z = 29.048, p < 0.001; Fig. 2A, Sanguino-Gómez et al., 2024: β = 4.412, SE = 0.191, z = 23.091, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). A significant ELS × time interaction was found in both datasets (β = −0.887, SE = 0.181, z = −4.898, p < 0.001; β = −0.721, SE = 0.27, z = −2.667, p = 0.008 respectively), indicating that ELS animals showed a slower increase in freezing over time. A combined analysis confirmed this interaction (β = −0.822, SE = 0.154, z = −5.327, p < 0.001; Fig. 2C), demonstrating that ELS blunts the progression of fear-related freezing. These findings show that our supervised classifier provides a reliable, scalable method for quantifying freezing and demonstrates that ELS blunts the progression of fear-related freezing over time.

## Unsupervised behavioral sequencing recapitulates the supervised freezing effect in ELS

After confirming that the supervised pipeline reliably scores freezing, we applied keypoint MoSeq¹⁷ to the same pose-estimation dataset to test whether unsupervised motifs aligned with supervised freezing labels. Keypoint MoSeq generated 96 behavioral syllables (Fig. 2D), which were reduced to 38 after frequency filtering. The retained syllables accounted for 99.5% of all labeled frames (Fig. 2D). When matched to supervised freezing labels, a single dominant syllable (S0) accounted for roughly 75% of all freezing frames. Applying a precision threshold above 50% (Fig. 2E), we included an additional syllable (S28) that, despite lower recall (Fig. 2F), contributed modestly to freezing. Visual inspection indicated that S0 resembles a classic, elongated freezing posture, while S28 is characterized by a more curled and compact posture resembling a huddling-like freezing form. Interestingly, we identified three syllables with high precision for freezing exclusive to the ELS animals (S68, S51 and S48, Fig. 2E), suggesting subtle, ELS-specific differences in freezing behavior. However, due to their low frequency, these syllables did not survive the filtering step and were excluded from downstream analysis. When combined, S0 and S28 overlapped with SimBA ground truth labels for about 81% of freezing frames per animal (Control: 81.5 ± 9.1%; ELS: 80.1 ± 10.4%; Fig. 2G), and every animal recapitulated more than 50% of manual freezing annotations (Fig. 2G). A mixed linear model on S0 + S28 usage replicated the supervised finding: a significant main effect of time (β = 0.126, SE = 0.004, z = 32.620, p < 0.001, Fig. 2H) and a significant ELS × time interaction (β = −0.023, SE = 0.005, z = −4.262, p < 0.001, Fig. 2H) indicating that, although both groups increased their use of these syllables, the rate was slower in ELS animals. These findings demonstrate that unsupervised phenotyping effectively recapitulates supervised results in freezing behavior.

## Unsupervised behavioral sequencing methods reveal new behavioral motifs altered by ELS

Once validated, we used keypoint MoSeq¹⁷ to identify new sets of behaviors during FC and assess how ELS alters their expression. Syllables were visually inspected and grouped into seven ethologically relevant clusters: freeze, sniff, groom, turn, locomotion, climb and jump (definitions in Supplementary Table 1), all of which were present in both groups across experiments and time periods.

Syllables with mixed behaviors or tracking inaccuracies were excluded (~20% of frames), with no differences between conditions (Supplementary Figure 1). Among the mapped behaviors, ELS animals showed fewer freeze events (ELS versus control: β = −0.204, SE = 0.081, z = −2.510, p = 0.012, BH_FDR p = 0.028; Fig. 3A), more sniff events (β = 0.621, SE = 0.231, z = 2.694, p = 0.007, BH_FDR p = 0.025; Fig. 3A) and more turn events (β = 0.101, SE = 0.032, z = 3.099, p = 0.002, BH_FDR p = 0.014; Fig. 3A). When examined over time (Fig. 3B-H), ELS animals showed a blunted freeze increase relative to controls (β = −0.023, SE = 0.005, z = −4.262, p < 0.001, BH_FDR p < 0.001; Fig. 3B), consistent with our SimBA-based findings (Fig. 2A-C). This approach further revealed that ELS offspring sniffed more at trial onset and, over time, normalized to control levels (β = −0.014, SE = 0.004, z = −3.656, p < 0.001, BH_FDR p < 0.001; Fig. 3C), sustained turning at higher levels than controls throughout the trial (β = 0.026, SE = 0.007, z = 3.610, p < 0.001, BH_FDR p < 0.001; Fig. 3E) and showed a smaller decline in locomotion relative to controls over the trial (β = 0.005, SE = 0.002, z = 2.553, p = 0.011, BH_FDR p = 0.019; Fig. 3F). Together, these findings indicate that ELS broadly alters the behavioral repertoire during fear conditioning.

## ELS reduces behavioral diversity in favor of prolonged active states

ELS may also alter the pattern in which behaviors occur. To assess this, we generated ethograms and behavioral barcodes for each animal, arranging behaviors from least to most active, and mapped all 82 animals into representative profiles per condition (Fig. 4A-D and Supplementary Material and Methods for profiles per animal). These ethograms revealed that ELS offspring showed a trend toward lower Simpson diversity that did not reach significance (β = −0.013, SE = 0.007, z = −1.915, p = 0.056; Fig. 4E), consistent with engagement in a somewhat narrower behavioral repertoire. Shannon entropy and evenness were unchanged (Fig. 4F-G), meaning that the relative distribution and uniformity of behaviors remained similar between groups. By contrast, ELS animals displayed a significantly higher cumulative usage index (CUI; β = 0.064, SE = 0.028, z = 2.250, p = 0.025; Fig. 4H-I), indicating that the most frequently used clusters accounted for a larger share of their overall activity. Thus, the concentration of activity into fewer clusters (CUI), rather than a significant reduction in Simpson diversity, points to a more restricted repertoire in ELS animals, without a change in behavioral evenness. At the bout level (Fig. 4J-Q), ELS animals exhibited cluster-specific changes: freezing bouts were shorter (β = −0.094, SE = 0.033, z = −2.824, p = 0.005, BH_FDR p = 0.016; Fig. 4K), while more active behaviors such as sniffing (β = 0.321, SE = 0.126, z = 2.540, p = 0.011, BH_FDR p = 0.026; Fig. 4L) and turning (β = 0.164, SE = 0.058, z = 2.824, p = 0.005, BH_FDR p = 0.016; Fig. 4N) showed prolonged bout durations. Altogether, ELS animals engaged in a more restricted behavioral repertoire characterized by prolonged active behaviors and reduced passive (freezing) bout duration.

## ELS reduces behavioral diversity in favor of stereotypy

We next focused on the transitions between behavioral states. The transition chordplots per group and the differences between groups are shown in Fig. 4R and S, respectively. While Lempel-Ziv complexity was unchanged (Fig. 4T), ELS animals showed a non-significant trend toward increased recurrence rate (β = 0.013, SE = 0.007, z = 1.908, p = 0.056, Fig. 4U) and a significant increase in determinism (β = 0.016, SE = 0.007, z = 2.167, p = 0.030, Fig. 4V), indicating that at least some behavioral sequences became more repetitive and predictable. We also quantified entropy by analyzing the transition probabilities between behavioral states using a first-order Markov chain. ELS animals showed a reduction in Markov entropy that did not reach significance (β = −0.041, SE = 0.025, z = −1.638, p = 0.102; Fig. 4W). Together, these transition metrics point to a modest shift toward more deterministic, less flexible sequences in ELS offspring, driven mainly by increased determinism, although the recurrence increase was only a trend and Markov entropy did not differ significantly. This pattern is consistent with somewhat more stereotyped behavior during FC, which may reflect reduced adaptability when faced with a secondary stressor or challenge.

## Individual behavioral dynamics scores identify resilient profiles in ELS animals

To further explore individual differences, we computed temporal behavioral profiles by binning behavior into 30-second intervals and calculating the relative frequency of each cluster over time (Supplementary Fig. 2). Next, pairwise Euclidean distance matrices were projected into two dimensions using multidimensional scaling (MDS; Supplementary Fig. 2), and from this space we derived a behavioral dynamics score as the log ratio of each animal's Euclidean distance to the ELS median versus the control median (Fig. 5A), paralleling the behavioral flow score¹⁶ while incorporating a temporal component. Notably, ELS animals scored higher on this metric (β = 0.336, SE = 0.087, z = 3.869, p < 0.001, Fig. 5B). The Euclidean distance achieved a leave-one-out cross-validation accuracy of 63.4% (Fig. 5A), and results were robust across alternative distance metrics including correlation, cosine, Minkowski and Manhattan distances (Supplementary Fig. 3A-H). Within the ELS group only, animals with behavioral dynamics scores below zero (indicating control-like behavioral profiles) were classified as "resilient." Out of 41 ELS animals, 12 met this criterion (6 from each cohort²⁴,²⁵), accounting for approximately 30% of the ELS population. The overlap of resilient classifications between the Euclidean method and other metrics ranged from 58% to 75% (Supplementary Materials and Methods). We also computed similarity scores based on transition probabilities (inspired by von Ziegler et al¹⁶), which showed 58.3% overlap with the dynamics-based classifications (Supplementary Fig. 3J-K). Using this new classification (control, vulnerable ELS and resilient ELS), we then reexamined behavior frequency across groups. Resilient ELS animals froze more than vulnerable ELS animals (β = 0.409, SE = 0.118, z = 3.476, p < 0.001, BH_FDR = 0.002; Fig. 5C) and were statistically comparable to controls. Likewise, resilient ELS animals turned less than vulnerable ELS animals (β = −0.145, SE = 0.033, z = −4.437, p < 0.001, BH_FDR < 0.001; Fig. 5C) and did not differ from controls. Over time (Fig. 5D-J), resilient animals exhibited a steeper freeze increase relative to vulnerable ELS (β = 1.865, SE = 0.249, z = 7.484, p < 0.001, BH_FDR p < 0.001; Fig. 5D) and even steeper than controls (β = 0.619, SE = 0.238, z = 2.599, p = 0.009, BH_FDR p = 0.065; Fig. 5D). They also showed decreased turn over time compared to vulnerable ELS (β = −1.622, SE = 0.326, z = −4.967, p < 0.001, BH_FDR p < 0.001; Fig. 5G) and less sniffing at trial onset (β = 0.338, SE = 0.170, z = 1.970, p = 0.047, BH_FDR p = 0.109; Fig. 5E).

Representative ethograms and barcodes for each condition are presented in Fig. 6A-F. Overall, differences between control and ELS animals were largely driven by the vulnerable subgroup: vulnerable ELS animals showed lower Simpson diversity than controls (β = −0.022, SE = 0.008, z = −2.835, p = 0.005; Fig. 6G) and a more limited repertoire than resilient animals (β = 0.029, SE = 0.011, z = 2.627, p = 0.009, Fig. 6G), while resilient animals resembled controls. There were no changes in Shannon entropy or evenness (Fig. 6H-I). At the bout level (Fig. 6L-S), resilient animals froze in longer bouts (β = 0.263, SE = 0.089, z = 2.969, p = 0.003, BH_FDR p = 0.021; Fig. 6M) and turned in shorter bouts (β = −0.201, SE = 0.089, z = −2.255, p = 0.024, BH_FDR p = 0.084; Fig. 6P) than vulnerable ELS animals, while vulnerable animals sniffed in longer bouts than controls (β = 0.420, SE = 0.128, z = 3.285, p = 0.001, BH_FDR p = 0.004; Fig. 6N), with resilient animals falling in between. Transition analysis across the three subgroups (Fig. 6T-V) revealed that vulnerable ELS animals showed reduced Lempel-Ziv complexity (β = −8.965, SE = 2.658, z = −3.37, p < 0.001, Fig. 6W), increased recurrence (β = 0.026, SE = 0.008, z = 2.798, p = 0.005; Fig. 6X), higher determinism (β = 0.010, SE = 0.003, z = 2.860, p = 0.004; Fig. 6Y), and lower Markov entropy (β = −0.044, SE = 0.022, z = −1.99, p = 0.046; Fig. 6Z) relative to controls, while resilient animals showed recurrence rates closer to controls and significantly lower than the vulnerable subgroup (β = −0.029, SE = 0.011, z = −2.727, p = 0.006; Fig. 6X).

In summary, by integrating multi-behavioral temporal dynamics through unsupervised methods, we identified a resilient subpopulation within the ELS group with distinct behavioral trajectories: prolonged passive behavior, shorter active responses, and a more diverse, flexible, and unpredictable repertoire that diverges from the stereotypical ELS pattern and aligns closer to controls.

## Interpretable machine learning validates behavioral clusters

To assess how well keypoint MoSeq-identified behaviors could be distinguished from pose dynamics alone and to build a generalizable tool for behavioral classification in new recordings, we trained an XGBoost classifier on egocentric pose-derived features, including keypoint positions, pairwise distances, heading angles, angular and centroidal velocities, and global body orientation, with temporal structure retained via rolling window statistics and time-lagged features (Supplementary Material and Methods). The classifier reached 62.7% overall accuracy with 3-fold stratified cross-validation, substantially above chance (12.5%; 5.0× gain, Fig. 7A). Accuracy varied across behaviors (Fig. 7A): freeze and climb were classified with highest accuracy (86.1% and 80.4%), followed by jump (70.6%), sniff (67.9%), and locomotion (61.5%), while groom (45.0%), turn (46.7%) and unassigned frames (30.1%) were harder to disambiguate, with misclassifications occurring predominantly among movement-rich motifs (Fig. 7C) (e.g. groom is often mistaken for unlabeled behavior and turn is occasionally defined as locomotion). These results suggest that while some motifs are clearly distinguishable based on pose dynamics alone, others may require additional contextual or temporal information for accurate disambiguation.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)²⁶ analysis identified body tilt variability, angular velocity, and global turning speed as the top discriminating features (Fig. 7B), with cluster-specific importance profiles reflecting distinct pose dynamics for each behavior: freeze was characterized by near-zero centroid speed and minimal lean variance; locomotion depended on rhythmic swings in limb-to-center distances; climb showed high variance in head-to-body distance; and jump was defined by sharp peaks in summed velocity and angular acceleration (Fig. 7D–K). These feature profiles aligned with the behavioral definitions used for visual classification of keypoint MoSeq syllables (Supplementary Table 1), validating the cluster assignments and confirming that the classifier captures behaviorally meaningful structure.

Crucially, once trained, this classifier can be applied directly to new DeepLabCut recordings to annotate the same behavioral repertoire without requiring keypoint MoSeq retraining, providing a scalable and interpretable pipeline for behavioral classification across datasets.
